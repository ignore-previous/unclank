# Year 9 Physics: Electric Circuits

Twelve single-best-answer questions for the end-of-unit quiz. Each item gives the key, an explanation that covers every option, and the feedback shown after an answer.

## Q1

A battery is joined to a bulb by two wires in one complete loop. How does the current leaving the bulb compare with the current entering it?

A. Smaller, as the bulb uses some current up
B. Equal, as no charge is used up
C. Larger, as the bulb adds energy to it
D. Half of it, with the rest turned into light

**Key:** B
**Explanation:** Charge is not used up as it flows, so the current is the same size all the way round a single loop. What the bulb transfers is energy, which leaves as light and heat. A is the common belief that the bulb consumes current. C mixes up current with energy. D invents a split that only happens at a junction, and there is none here.
**Feedback if correct:** Charge keeps flowing at the same rate round the loop, and the energy it carries is what the bulb transfers.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Separate the two ideas. Current is a flow of charge, and it is not used up. Energy is what the charge hands over in the bulb.

## Q2

Two identical bulbs are in series with a battery. A third identical bulb is added in series with them. What happens to the first two bulbs?

A. They get brighter, because the new bulb helps the battery
B. They stay as bright, because the battery sets the current
C. They go out, because the new bulb takes the current
D. They get dimmer, because the resistance has gone up

**Key:** D
**Explanation:** The battery gives a fixed potential difference, not a fixed current. More resistance in the loop means a smaller current, so each bulb is dimmer. A treats extra components as extra help. B treats the battery as a source of constant current. C treats the new bulb as a thief that keeps the current to itself.
**Feedback if correct:** The battery supplies a set voltage, so more resistance in the loop gives a smaller current.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Ask what the battery fixes. It fixes the voltage, so the current has to change when the resistance changes.

## Q3

Two identical bulbs are in parallel across a battery. The filament in one bulb breaks. What happens to the other bulb?

A. It stays as bright as it was before
B. It goes out, since the whole circuit is now broken
C. It gets dimmer while the battery works harder
D. It gets brighter by taking the broken bulb's share

**Key:** A
**Explanation:** Each branch of a parallel circuit is its own path across the battery, so the working bulb keeps the same voltage and the same current. B applies series thinking to a parallel circuit. C imagines the battery being strained by the gap. D treats current as a fixed supply that is handed on to the next bulb.
**Feedback if correct:** Each branch connects straight across the battery, so one branch does not affect another.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Trace the path from one battery terminal to the other through the working bulb. It does not pass through the broken one.

## Q4

A student wants to measure the current through a lamp. Which meter should they use, and how should they connect it?

A. An ammeter, in series with the lamp
B. An ammeter, in parallel with the lamp
C. A voltmeter, in series with the lamp
D. A voltmeter, in parallel with the lamp

**Key:** A
**Explanation:** The current has to pass through the meter to be counted, so an ammeter goes in series. B picks the right meter but connects it across the lamp, where it would not count the lamp's current. C and D pick a voltmeter, which compares two points and does not measure current; D is how you would measure the lamp's voltage.
**Feedback if correct:** The ammeter sits in the loop so the charge flows through it.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Decide the meter first, then the connection. A voltmeter compares two points, so it goes across a component. An ammeter counts the flow, so it goes in line.

## Q5

A 6 V battery is joined across two identical resistors in series. What is the potential difference across each resistor?

A. 6 V
B. 2 V
C. 3 V
D. 12 V

**Key:** C
**Explanation:** Identical resistors in series share the battery's potential difference equally, and 6 V divided by 2 gives 3 V each. A gives each resistor the full battery voltage, which is true of parallel branches. B divides by 3, as if there were another component. D adds the voltages, which counts the battery twice.
**Feedback if correct:** The two voltages across the resistors add up to the battery voltage.
**Feedback if incorrect:** In series the battery voltage is shared out between the components. Count the components and divide.

## Q6

What carries the current in a metal wire?

A. Positive ions moving along the metal from the battery
B. Free electrons that are already in the metal
C. Protons travelling from the positive terminal
D. Electrons stored in the battery that flow out

**Key:** B
**Explanation:** A metal contains free electrons throughout. The battery's push reaches the whole loop almost at once, so the bulb lights straight away even though each electron drifts slowly. D is the belief that the battery is a tank of electrons. A and C treat positive particles as the movers, but the ions and protons stay fixed in the solid.
**Feedback if correct:** The wire is already full of free electrons, and the battery sets them moving together.
**Feedback if incorrect:** The battery does not supply the charge carriers. It gives the electrons already in the metal a push.

## Q7

A resistor has a potential difference of 12 V across it and a current of 3 A through it. What is its resistance?

A. 36 ohms
B. 15 ohms
C. 0.25 ohms
D. 4 ohms

**Key:** D
**Explanation:** Resistance is potential difference divided by current, so 12 V divided by 3 A gives 4 ohms. A multiplies the two numbers, which gives power in watts and not resistance. B adds them. C divides the current by the voltage, which turns the equation upside down.
**Feedback if correct:** Resistance equals potential difference divided by current.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Write the equation first: resistance equals potential difference divided by current. Then check which value goes on top.

## Q8

A plastic comb is rubbed with wool and then picks up small scraps of paper. What happened during the rubbing?

A. Protons rubbed off the wool onto the comb
B. The rubbing turned the comb into a magnet
C. Electrons moved between the comb and the wool
D. Friction made new charge on the comb from heat

**Key:** C
**Explanation:** Electrons are the charged particles that can move from one insulator to another, so one material gains electrons and the other loses them. A fails because protons are fixed in the nuclei. B confuses static electricity with magnetism. D treats rubbing as a charge factory, but charge is moved and not made.
**Feedback if correct:** Rubbing moves electrons between the surfaces, and the total charge stays the same.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Ask which particles can move and whether charge can be created. Charge is only transferred.

## Q9

A 2 kW kettle runs for 3 minutes. How much energy does it transfer?

A. 360 000 J
B. 6 000 J
C. 120 000 J
D. 2 000 J

**Key:** A
**Explanation:** Energy equals power times time. Using 2 000 W and 180 s gives 360 000 J. B multiplies by 3 and forgets to turn minutes into seconds. C uses 60 s, which is one minute and not three. D gives the power itself, which is a rate and not an amount of energy.
**Feedback if correct:** Energy is power multiplied by time, with the time in seconds.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Convert to base units before you multiply: watts for power and seconds for time.

## Q10

A fault in a toaster lets 30 A flow through its cable. The plug has a 13 A fuse. What does the fuse do?

A. It lowers the mains voltage to a safe level
B. The current is held steady at 13 A
C. It sends the extra current down the earth wire
D. Its thin wire melts and breaks the circuit

**Key:** D
**Explanation:** A fuse contains a thin wire that heats and melts when the current exceeds its rating, which breaks the circuit before the cable overheats. A describes a transformer. B describes a regulator, which a plug does not contain. C is the job of the earth wire, not the fuse.
**Feedback if correct:** 30 A is well over the 13 A rating, so the fuse wire melts and opens the circuit.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Match each job to the part that does it. A fuse protects by breaking the circuit, and it does not change or steady anything.

## Q11

In a house, a lamp and a TV are plugged into two different sockets. The TV is switched off at the wall. Why does the lamp stay on?

A. The TV was using only a small share of the current
B. Each one is on its own branch across the mains
C. Switching off sends the TV's current to the lamp instead
D. The lamp's cable stores enough charge to keep it lit

**Key:** B
**Explanation:** House sockets are wired in parallel, so each appliance has its own branch across the full mains voltage and switching one off leaves the others working. A applies series thinking: in a series loop, any switch turned off would stop everything, whatever its share. C treats current as a fixed supply that is handed on. D treats a cable as a store of charge.
**Feedback if correct:** Parallel branches give each appliance the full voltage and let each be switched on its own.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Picture the lamp and TV in one series loop. What would switching off the TV do there, and how is the house different?

## Q12

A student turns a dimmer knob so that the resistance of a circuit goes up. The battery voltage stays the same. What happens to the current?

A. It rises, as a bigger resistance pushes harder
B. It stays the same while the voltage is unchanged
C. It falls, as there is more opposition to flow
D. It rises once charge piles up behind the resistor

**Key:** C
**Explanation:** Current equals voltage divided by resistance, so with the voltage fixed a larger resistance gives a smaller current. A treats resistance as a push. B assumes that a fixed voltage means a fixed current. D pictures charge building up and then rushing on, but the current is the same at every point in a loop.
**Feedback if correct:** With a fixed voltage, current is inversely related to resistance.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Use current equals voltage divided by resistance. Keep the voltage the same and see what a larger bottom number does.
