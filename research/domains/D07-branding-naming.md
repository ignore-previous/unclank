# D07 branding-naming

Product and company names, domains, logos, icons and taglines that read as AI-generated or low-effort. Round 1 (breadth) and round 2 (depth) merged. Most naming and logo sources are vendors (name generators, domain sellers, logo studios, copy-tool sellers), and vendor pages that repeat each other count as one source. No study of which product or brand names LLMs suggest was found. The one measured study of repeated LLM names covers fiction (Hamilton and Mimno, Cornell, 2026: character names, settings and professions, S-D07-026); it shows the mechanism but not the product-name set, so the name-set tell stays anecdotal. The visual-sameness critique is mostly opinion; the solid data points are the NN/g sparkle study (n=107), which conflicts with Google's own research, and a 2023 trademark-filing count of hexagons in AI logos that was seen only in a search summary. Round 2 retired the suffix tell (D07-008): the -ly/-fy trend dates to about 2008 to 2013 and is now a Not a tell item (D07-N04); only the "-AI" ending was folded into D07-003. Practical risk note: trademark and domain collisions are a real launch risk for any new name (a July 2026 suit between two AI startups with the same brand name is an example, S-D07-022), but no source shows that AI naming causes them, so they are not listed as a tell. Run a trademark and domain check on any name before committing, however it was chosen. A thin source (iprightshub, S-D07-023, search only, unverified) claims name generators rarely check trademark databases.

## Checklist
- [Choose deliberately · moderate] Keep the four-point sparkle only with a text label that says what the feature does; otherwise draw a glyph tied to the feature (D07-001)
- [Choose deliberately · moderate] For an AI-company logo, avoid the default round gradient orb or swirl; use a flat geometric mark with a reason behind its shape (D07-002)
- [Avoid · moderate] Replace "Verb-ing X, Verb-ing Y" taglines with one concrete thing the product does for one named user (D07-004)
- [Choose deliberately · moderate] Choose a .ai domain only for a product built on AI; otherwise use the brand's own .com or a neutral extension (D07-005)

## Tells

### D07-001 Sparkle icon as the default AI symbol
- Label: Choose deliberately
- Confidence: moderate (2 independent sources that disagree, plus 1 press piece seen only in a search result; one has n=107, the other about 2,000 participants)
- Dated: noted from 2024 (NN/g, 20 Sep 2024; Google research undated), still cited in Dec 2025 (Slate)
- Why it reads as AI: The sparkle is the stock "AI feature" glyph in software, so it signals a template choice, not a product-specific one. Google reported close to 100 different Sparkle icons in use in 2024. The evidence conflicts: NN/g found users did not tie the icon to AI (about 17% read it as "favourite or save"; 73% read a plain star that way), while Google's research found a sparkle was enough to signal AI, especially in context.
- Fix: Pair it with a text label. The pairing matters more than the shape: Google found a sparkle beside a mic signals AI better than a mic alone, and NN/g advises always adding text.
- Fixable: yes by prompting/editing (swap the glyph or add a text label)
- See also: D03-009
- Sources: S-D07-001, S-D07-002, S-D07-003
- Flag: Sources conflict (NN/g tested the icon outside its product context; Google tested Google products only and sells the feature). The tell is default use, not the shape. Slate is search only (headline and date seen, body not read).

### D07-002 Gradient orb, swirl, hexagon or hollow-circle mark for AI companies
- Label: Choose deliberately
- Confidence: moderate (opinion pieces from a humorous blogger and an agency owner, both fetched, plus search-only press, forum and a 2023 trademark count; no fetched measurement. Held at moderate in round 2 because the only measurement concerns hexagons, not orbs, and was not read) Lead review 20 Oct (r2): held at moderate. The non-vendor items are opinion pieces, and the only measurement (hexagons, 4.6x) is unread.
- Dated: noted from 2023 (Fast Company and Emblemetric, Apr 2023, "swirling hexagon"), Mar to Apr 2025 (Creative Bloq, VelvetShark), 19 Aug 2026 (Pratt, The Drum), still cited in 2026
- Why it reads as AI: AI-company logos cluster on a round or spherical form with a gradient, a central void and radiating curves, so the mark reads as copied from the market leaders or from a generator trained on them. Critics call it corporate uniformity; Rob Pratt (The Drum, 2026) writes "please do not add another circular gradient blob to our lives". A 2023 trademark analysis reportedly found hexagons about 4.6 times more likely in AI logos than in logos overall (search summary only).
- Fix: Build the mark from flat geometric shapes, use negative space that means something about the product, drop the gradient and radial symmetry, and check the mark in one colour. A hexagon or circle is fine if it means something about the product. These fixes are the authors' opinions.
- Fixable: yes by prompting/editing (ask for flat geometric construction with no gradient, then check in one colour)
- Sources: S-D07-004, S-D07-005, S-D07-006, S-D07-031, S-D07-033, S-D07-034, S-D07-035, S-D07-048
- Flag: Fetched: VelvetShark (its author calls it humorous opinion), Pratt, and the Emblemetric teaser. Pratt co-owns the agency YeahNice and cites no data, so treat him as interested opinion. The Emblemetric teaser gives no figures or method. The 4.6x hexagon figure and the "over 99% of filings after 2015" detail come only from search summaries of Fast Company (full article returned 403), and Morning Brew repeats the same figure (one origin); keep search only. The figure is for hexagons from 2023 filings, not orbs or gradients. Creative Bloq is a search snippet (body truncated; do not cite for any quote). Ebaq (S-D07-006) is a design-studio blog, date not seen; its count of 22 startups is unverified and was only seen repeated second-hand in a DesignRush snippet. Indie Hackers is a forum comment about an "AI purple" look, search only. Counter-voice: Tom Banks (The Drum, Feb 2025, search only) called the OpenAI symbol "rather neat" (see D07-N03). Round 1 named DeepSeek and Midjourney as exceptions per VelvetShark; round 2 could not confirm that in the source, so the example was removed.

### D07-003 Name families that LLMs keep suggesting (Nova, Nexus, Lumina, Synapse, Flux, Sol)
- Label: Choose deliberately
- Confidence: anecdotal (lead review 20 Oct: downgraded from moderate, and kept there in round 2. For product names the evidence is two naming vendors counted as one plus a headline nobody opened. The character-name studies below show that LLMs converge on a small name pool, but they do not measure product names. D06 has the better-evidenced fiction-name set.)
- Dated: lists seen across 2025 to 2026 (Nymly 19 Apr 2026, The Nameist 30 Jun 2026, TechRadar 16 Aug 2026 per a search summary); fiction-name repetition noted from Aug 2023 (Elara, per Max Read 7 Aug 2025), measured May 2026 (Hamilton and Mimno), Cornell release 19 Oct 2026
- Why it reads as AI: Generated shortlists drift toward the same few words (Nova, Nexus, Lumina, Synapse, Flux, Sol, Aurum, Lux, Aura, Vellum). One naming consultant argues that a model picks from the middle of all good names, which is a mechanism claim, not a measurement. Mechanism evidence from fiction: Hamilton and Mimno (Cornell, 26 May 2026) generated 20,000 stories from four models (Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.4-Mini, OLMo 7B Thinking) and found Elias in 26.5% of them, Mara in 16.7% and Elara in 13.1%, with name preferences differing by model. The paper does not study product or brand names. Nymly's list also flags descriptive tech words (Neural, Synth, Brain, Mind, Core, Lab, "AI-" prefixes); these are crowded and, per Nymly, weak as trademarks. Sub-point, the "-AI" ending: a name that ends or starts with AI reads as a generator default, and one vendor (Lengreo) says it now signals a thin wrapper around someone else's model.
- Fix: Name from the product's own world (a place, a material, a user's phrase), or use a real word from an unrelated field, or a coined word with a hard consonant. Check the candidate against trademark and domain databases, and against the names LLMs keep returning, before committing. If the name could replace a competitor's name without changing the sentence, pick another. Because name preferences differ by model, a list from one model may not match another.
- See also: D06
- Sources: S-D07-010, S-D07-011, S-D07-012, S-D07-013, S-D07-015, S-D07-026, S-D07-027, S-D07-028, S-D07-029, S-D07-030, S-D07-037, S-D07-040, S-D07-047
- Flag: No study of LLM product-name output was found, so the product claim is anecdotal. The Nameist post (author is a naming consultant who sells naming services) gives no prompts or run counts, and its "statistical centre" claim was not rechecked. Nymly (naming vendor, fetched in round 2) gives no method: it cites seed-stage Crunchbase companies and "a recent Y Combinator batch" without counts or dates, its only count is "roughly 400 active brand names" for Flux, "hundreds" for Nova has no exact figure, and its "fifteen-word vocabulary" lists only 12 words; its tech-word list (Brain, Mind, Core, Lab) was not rechecked. The Nameist and Nymly are naming vendors (one source). TechRadar is a headline only (body truncated on fetch). Morningstar lists Polaris and NEXUS to avoid but does not tie them to AI. The Creative Bloq claim that names end in AI, ly or a vowel is a search snippet (body unreadable). Fiction evidence: Hamilton and Mimno, the Cornell release (which calls WildChat a likely origin, not proven), Namerology (Laura Wattenberg; observation only, and her own test of three chatbots over five prompts gave Elara once in 15 names, which counters the claim) and TODAY give no product-name data; Max Read counts 62 Amazon books with Elara-type names plus "hundreds" of self-published titles, with no systematic count, and expects the name to fade in later models. TomLikesRobots (X post, 5 LLMs x 1,000 openings) and tanj.co are search only; their counts are unverified and tanj.co gives none. Lengreo is a vendor (search only). A registry vendor's figure (.TECH, S-D07-043, search only, fetch returned 403) says 84.2% of AI-related sites lack "ai" in the name, so the -AI ending is not the norm. A named word is not a tell if it was chosen for a reason; Nova is also a real given name. The -ly and -fy endings are not part of this tell; see D07-N04.

### D07-004 Vague "Verb-ing X, Verb-ing Y" tagline
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (TinyTechGuides plus one group of vendor pages, all search only; round 2 added two more sellers of AI copy tools, which do not raise it; the AI-authorship link rests on one anecdote)
- Dated: noticed 2025 to 2026 (Jenova.ai page Apr 2026)
- Why it reads as AI: Lines such as "Empowering Innovation" or "Simplifying AI, Empowering Growth" pair abstract verbs with abstract nouns, so they say nothing about what the product does or for whom, and they could sit on any product. Rob Palmer says AI copy gravitates toward the average, smooth and generic.
- Fix: State one concrete thing the product does for one named user, in plain words. Test whether a competitor could use the same line.
- See also: D01
- Sources: S-D07-019, S-D07-020, S-D07-021, S-D07-038, S-D07-049
- Flag: All five sources are search only. Hix.ai and Salesforce are vendor pages (counted as one). Palmer and Jenova.ai both sell AI copy services or tools, so they are not independent voices. Jenova quotes Palmer on a "tenfold" figure and "smooth-but-empty sentences" that were not found on any Palmer page; do not cite that figure. Round 2 found no independent designer or press critique of "Empowering" lines. "Empowering" was a corporate cliche before LLMs, so the word alone does not show AI authorship; the test is specificity. Branding angle only; the wider buzzword and copy tells belong to D01.

### D07-005 ".ai" as the whole brand for a product that is not AI
- Label: Choose deliberately
- Confidence: moderate (1 vendor plus 1 press piece, both search only; the weakest of the moderate tells. Round 2 found no independent designer voice on .ai as a hype signal) Lead review 20 Oct (r2): held at moderate as the minimum (two independent but search-only sources). Drop to anecdotal if round 3 can't fetch either one.
- Dated: commentary from Apr 2025; growth figures 2023 to 2026
- Why it reads as AI: The .ai extension became a default AI marker in 2024 to 2025. On a product not built on AI it reads as hype, and it tracks the generator-style "-AI" name more than a product decision.
- Fix: Choose the domain for the brand, not for the signal. If the product is not built on AI, use .com or a neutral extension. If it is, say on the page what the AI does.
- Sources: S-D07-016, S-D07-017, S-D07-018
- Flag: All search only. Snagged and Domain-ate are domain vendors (one source). TechStartups says the move from .ai to .com "isn't a flood". The YC .ai percentages in round 1 (23% for W24, 28% for H1 2025) are a vendor figure and unverified; a search result attributes the 28% figure to Identity Digital (the registry operator) for newly founded tech startups, not to YC, so the two should not be treated as one figure. Independent press found in round 2 (Ars Technica 2023, S-D07-039; Forbes India, S-D07-044; PYMNTS, S-D07-045; all search only) covers .ai as a revenue and speculation story, not as a taste signal. Hacker News search returned no critique. For a real AI product this is not a tell (see D07-N02).

### D07-006 Generated raster mark that breaks at small sizes
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: anecdotal (1 studio blog; other pages are vendor blogs with the same advice)
- Dated: 2025 to 2026; Left Hand Design 19 Oct 2026
- Why it reads as AI: Generated marks are often detailed rasters with gradients, soft shadows and mockup lighting. They look good in a presentation and fail as a 16 to 32 px favicon, a one-colour print or an avatar. The failure shows that nobody checked the mark at real size.
- Fix: Test the bare flat mark in black on white and white on black, at 16, 32 and 512 px, and in one colour. Ask for vector masters, a one-colour version and a simplified small-size version. Rebuild any part that fails.
- Fixable: yes by prompting/editing for the flat version; partly for the vector file (a vectorised raster often has bloated paths)
- Sources: S-D07-007
- Flag: Left Hand Design is a studio that sells logo services, and the same page says AI authorship cannot be proved from appearance and a hurried human can make the same faults. Gradients are not the problem; legibility at small size is the test. The SVG Genie and GitHub brand-kit issue #76 mentions in the raw file have no URL and are not listed.

### D07-007 Stock symbols with no link to the business
- Label: Choose deliberately
- Confidence: anecdotal (1 studio blog plus 2 vendor blogs, one seen only in a search snippet; all vendors)
- Dated: sameness critique 2025 to 2026; Left Hand Design 19 Oct 2026; MalbarDesign 31 Aug 2026 (updated 5 Oct 2026)
- Why it reads as AI: A vague brief such as "modern tech" pushes generators toward shields, leaves, rings, monograms, centred badges, circuit lines and infinity loops. These show up across unrelated companies and match what a stock icon library gives anyone.
- Fix: Write a brief (audience, promise, competitors, what to avoid looking like). Base the symbol on something specific to the business, such as a form, a behaviour, a place or a point of view. Audit the mark against competitors with the mockup removed.
- Fixable: partly (the brief and concept need a human; prompting can only narrow the result)
- Sources: S-D07-008, S-D07-009, S-D07-032
- Flag: All three are logo vendors, so they count as one source. The Left Hand page cites no data; its only example is the studio's own project. It says these devices "aren't inherently bad". MalbarDesign (fetched in round 2) repeats the sameness argument with unattributed quotes and no data; it is opinion only. No logo similarity study was found.

### D07-008 Suffix patterns: -ify, -ly, -io, -ai (retired)
- Retired: round 2, 20 Oct 2026. The ID is kept so old references still resolve; do not reuse it.
- Why retired: The -ly, -fy and -me naming trend is dated to about 2008 to 2013 by a fetched 2013 article (The Next Web), so it cannot show that a name was generated. The trend moved to Not a tell as D07-N04. The AI-specific part, the "-AI" ending, was folded into D07-003 as a sub-point.
- Sources: S-D07-011, S-D07-014, S-D07-015

## Not a tell

### D07-N01 Short single-word or coined names (Linear, Cursor, Stripe, Notion)
- Why it is fine: Real words from an unrelated field, or coined words with a hard consonant, are common in well-regarded tech brands. Short and punchy alone is no evidence of AI.
- Sources: S-D07-024, S-D07-025, S-D07-011
- Flag: All sources are naming vendors and search only. The Nymly mention of Cursor, Lovable and Linear as good names is from a search snippet; the fetched page was not rechecked for it.

### D07-N02 .ai domain for a real AI product
- Why it is fine: For a product built on AI, the .ai extension is its natural home. The tell is the extension as the whole brand on a non-AI product (D07-005).
- Sources: S-D07-016, S-D07-018, S-D07-046
- Flag: Registry-growth figures differ by source and none is fetched: about 248,000 .ai domains in June 2023 to over 610,000 by early 2025 (round 1, search summaries); over one million by Jan 2026 (Nominus, vendor, search only); about 1.28 million in June 2026 (a search summary of an encyclopedia entry, no URL pinned). No source page was pinned for the first figures, so no URL is listed for them.

### D07-N03 Circle or abstract geometric marks made by human designers
- Why it is fine: Circles and abstract marks are not a tell in themselves. VelvetShark cites Slack, Netflix, Stripe, Twitch, FedEx and Apple as good models. Tom Banks (The Drum, Feb 2025) found the OpenAI symbol "rather neat". The shape is fine when chosen for a reason (D07-002).
- Sources: S-D07-004, S-D07-036
- Flag: The VelvetShark list of good models was not rechecked in round 2, when the DeepSeek and Midjourney example from the same source could not be confirmed. The Banks quote is from a search snippet only.

### D07-N04 -ly, -fy and -me suffix names
- Why it is fine: Suffix-family startup names (Bitly and About.me are named as pioneers) are a habit from about 2008 to 2013, long before generative AI, so using one does not show AI authorship. It can still be a weak choice for distinctiveness, but that is a different point.
- Sources: S-D07-014, S-D07-041, S-D07-042
- Flag: The Next Web (Ghoshal, 19 Oct 2013) is fetched and is the only source that dates the trend. Crunchbase News (2019) rests on consultant comments, gives no counts and says it "isn't a scientific verdict". CB Insights (2015) tracks domain extensions, not name endings, and gives no name-suffix counts, so it adds little. Some vendor lists still treat -ly as an AI-era habit, with no pre-AI comparison.
