# D09 educational-content

AI-written quiz items, multiple-choice questions (MCQs), flashcards, and the feedback and explanations that go with them. Most evidence is from item-quality studies in medical and higher education, so each tell carries a Data line (sample size, model tested, key figure) and names the model, so readers can judge how current it is. Most item-quality studies tested ChatGPT-3.5, GPT-4, Copilot (GPT-4 based), ChatGPT-4o or Mixtral-8x7B; treat their rates as a guide to what to check, not as rates for 2026 models. No fetched 2025-26 generator study (GPT-5, Claude, Gemini 2.5 or later) measured item-flaw rates or longest-option rates; the only one found, Bialik and Cummings 2026 (gpt-4.1-nano, gemini-2.5-flash), was seen as a search summary with no per-flaw rates. The fetched 2025-26 generator data are on answer-key position, Bloom level (one GPT-5 study), flashcards and praise in general chat. Rounds 1, 2 and 3 merged. Press and voices: Chalkbeat, EdSurge, Times Higher Education (round 2) and EdWeek, The 74, Tes, Chalkbeat and Times Higher Education again (round 3) were searched; nothing on quiz items from press, teachers or students was found. This is an open gap, and no tell comes from press or teacher voices. Tone and teacher reactions still have no direct quiz study.

## Checklist
- [Avoid · strong] Trace every answer key to a source passage and have a human verify it; never ship AI keys unchecked (D09-010)
- [Avoid · moderate] Reject items with two defensible options or no clearly correct option; test each distractor against the stem and source (D09-004)
- [Avoid · moderate] Shuffle option order in code after generation and reject batches with a skewed key distribution (D09-003)
- [Avoid · moderate] Reword the key so it does not repeat the stem's words, and strip hints and absolute terms ("always", "never") from stems and keys (D09-002)
- [Avoid · moderate] Match option length and specificity; flag any item where the key is the longest option (D09-001)
- [Avoid · moderate] Set a Bloom level per item, require scenarios for apply and analyse, and check the level mix and difficulty after a live trial (D09-007)
- [Avoid · moderate] After a pilot run, check item discrimination and rewrite or drop low-discrimination items; do not judge AI items on difficulty alone (D09-014)
- [Avoid · moderate] Write distractors from known student misconceptions; after a live run, rewrite any option chosen by under 5% of students (D09-005)
- [Avoid · moderate] Make each explanation name the misconception behind the learner's wrong answer, not a general walkthrough (D09-009)
- [Avoid · moderate] One fact per flashcard; reject cards that need the source text, accept several answers, or run wordy; spot-check for invented facts (D09-013)
- [Avoid · moderate] Cut praise openers from quiz feedback, especially on open-ended answers; say why the key is right and name the learner's specific error (D09-008)

## Tells

### D09-001 Correct answer is the longest option
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (1 fetched study with good data, 1 snippet-only study plus 1 snippet-only review; one fetched study found none, so the evidence is mixed. Round 3 added no fetched support.)
- Dated: noted from 2024 (ChatGPT-era studies), still cited in 2026; the longest-option cue itself is older item-writing guidance. Frontiers items came from Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, Hong Kong items from ChatGPT-4o and Springer items from zero-shot GPT models (versions not recovered). No GPT-5, Claude or Gemini 2.5+ longest-option rate has been measured.
- Why it reads as AI: the model writes a precise, hedged key and short, vaguer distractors, so a test-wise student can pick the key by length.
- Fix: write distractors at the same length and specificity as the key, and have a script flag items where the key is the longest option.
- Data: Frontiers (Menze, Radović, Seidel): 19 teachers, 152 items (63 teacher-only, 66 AI-only, 23 teacher-AI), generator Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1; key was the longest option in 34.9% (22 of 63) / 57.6% (38 of 66) / 82.6% (19 of 23) of items; Cramér's V 0.333, significant after Holm-Bonferroni (table checked in round 2). Flaws were rated by two LLMs (gpt-5.1-codex-mini, gemini-2.5-flash) and counted only when both agreed. Round 1 reported that a 30-item human subset disagreed with the LLM ratings 16% of the time (mean kappa 0.344 between the LLMs, 0.234 between the humans); round 2 could not verify the 16% and found that the human-rated subset gave a non-significant ANOVA (p = 0.145). Counts as one study. Grévisse, Pavlou & Schneider 2024 (SN Computer Science 5(5):636; zero-shot GPT models, versions not recovered): over-long keys were a common flaw in computer science (13 items) but occurred once among 100 endocrinology and neurology items; the authors suggest medical distractors (symptoms, doses) are about as long as the key. Summary seen via a citing paper only. Round 1's "150 computer-science questions" is not confirmed. Systematic review: lists "longest answer is correct" as a common flaw (counts not recovered, snippet only). Counter: Hong Kong cohort (Law et al. 2025, fetched), ChatGPT-4o, 100 items per set, found 0 longest-option items in both AI and human sets. Round 3: Bialik and Cummings (Springer 2026, gpt-4.1-nano and gemini-2.5-flash; search only) list longest-answer cueing as one of five checked flaws, the first 2025-26 generator study to do so, but the abstract gives no per-flaw rate; the gatherer worked out overall flawed-question rates of about 30.8% (gemini-2.5-flash) and 44.9% (gpt-4.1-nano) from stated averages of 69.2% and 55.1% unflawed, and it is not recovered whether those are baseline rates. Ould Ismail et al. 2026 (fetched; GPT-4 via ChatGPT Pro, 260 clinical-pathology MCQs) reports no longest-option count, and its prompt told the model that the key must not be the longest option.
- Sources: S-D09-001, S-D09-002, S-D09-003, S-D09-004, S-D09-044, S-D09-047
- Flag: evidence is mixed (Hong Kong found none; Springer found it in computer science but not medicine). The Frontiers rates rest on LLM raters with weak human agreement; a 2026 study of Gemini 2.5 Flash and Claude Sonnet 4 as MCQ raters found poor agreement on subjective flaws (S-D09-041, search only). The Springer, review and Bialik sources were seen only as search snippets or second-hand.

### D09-002 Stem-to-key cues: repeated words, logical hints, absolute terms
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (2 fetched studies, 1 more snippet-only; the Hong Kong data is small)
- Dated: noted with ChatGPT-3.5 items (2023-2024), still present in 2025-2026 studies. Mixtral-8x7B and ChatGPT-4o results; no GPT-5, Claude or Gemini 2.5+ cue rate has been measured.
- Why it reads as AI: the model tends to reuse the stem's own vocabulary when it writes the key, which hands the student a cue.
- Fix: reword the key with synonyms, remove absolute terms and logical hints from stems and keys, and read each stem for a clue to the answer.
- Data: Frontiers (19 teachers, 152 items, Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, LLM-rated): stem-key word repeats in 34.9% teacher-only / 80.3% AI-only / 73.9% teacher-AI items (Cramér's V = 0.443, the largest effect in the rubric); logical cues 25.4% / 56.1% / 65.2%; absolute terms 20.6% / 54.5% / 52.2%. Same rater caveat as D09-001. Hong Kong (Law et al. 2025, ChatGPT-4o, 100 items per set, 24 analysed participants): 6 AI items with word repeats and 6 with logical cues, against 0 and 0 in the human set. Cureus (ChatGPT, 45 gross-anatomy and embryology items, 15 NBME-style parameters): repeated stem and answer words in 15% of items (snippet only). Advances in Medical Education and Practice 2026 (Mitra et al.; Gemini Advanced, Perplexity Pro, ChatGPT 4.0; 21 items per tool per discipline; versions not given): experts scored "correct option not cued" 2.95 to 3.52 across tools, but no cue rate was reported and rater agreement on this criterion was near zero (Kendall's W = 0.067), so it is not counted as support. Bialik and Cummings (Springer 2026; search only) also check absolute terms, with no rate in the abstract.
- Sources: S-D09-001, S-D09-004, S-D09-005, S-D09-036, S-D09-044
- Flag: the teacher-only baseline also has 34.9% word repeats, so the habit is not AI-only; AI-only rates are higher. The Cureus source was seen only as a snippet and its date was not recovered.

### D09-003 Answer-key position skew
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (lead review 20 Oct: downgraded from strong; 2 independent fetched studies covering many models. The third round-1 source, a Medical Teacher key-at-C figure, could not be found in that paper, so the 3-source bar is not met.) Lead review 20 Oct (r2): downgrade to moderate accepted. The 59% vs 32% figure is not in its cited paper. Round 3: no third independent academic origin found; stays moderate.
- Dated: measured in 2026 papers on current open and closed models; still current.
- Why it reads as AI: each model family has fixed letter preferences, so keys cluster in some positions and D is underused, which students can learn.
- Fix: shuffle option order in code after generation, then check the key distribution across the batch and reject skewed batches. This is our own instruction, not the papers'; neither paper recommends shuffling, and a "keep it balanced" prompt alone is not reliable.
- Data: Tang, Duan & Cai (arXiv 2605.01846, May 2026; fetched): 10 text LLMs (including Gemini-2.5-Flash, Gemini-3-Pro, DeepSeek-Reasoner R1-0528, DeepSeek-Chat V3-0324, Qwen3-30B Thinking and Instruct, Llama 3.x, Mistral-7B) and 5 vision-language models (including Gemini-3-Pro); all skewed answer position (p < 0.001). Per round 1: Llama toward A, Qwen3 and DeepSeek toward B, Gemini toward B and C; D was underused. Removing option labels moved keys toward position 1 in 9 of 10 models (odds ratio about 1.76). Explicit balancing instructions helped, and DeepSeek-Reasoner and Gemini-3-Pro showed near-zero bias on two of the tasks. Item counts are inconsistent in the paper (250 vs 200 per discipline; 200 fits the prompt best). "Large Language Models Are Bad Dice Players" (arXiv 2601.05414 v3, Jan 2026; read via arXiv HTML in round 2): 1,000 medical MCQs per model at temperature 1.0 on GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3.2, Qwen3-32B, Llama-3.3-70B, Llama-4-Scout and GPT-OSS-120B; every prompt already asked for an even spread of answers, and there was no run without it. Key shares (A/B/C/D): GPT-4o 12.6 / 46.8 / 35.1 / 5.5; GPT-OSS-120B 4.5 / 27.7 / 54.6 / 13.2; all p < 0.001. Not counted: a practitioner blog (dev.to, search only) found an AI-written 300-question exam bank put the correct answer one position later in 58% of adjacent pairs, and shuffling options after generation brought a 120-question set to 32%; one anecdote, not independent academic evidence.
- Sources: S-D09-006, S-D09-007, S-D09-052
- Flag: round 1's third source (Medical Teacher 2025, "GPT-4 key at C in 59% of items vs 32% of experts", S-D09-008) is not in that paper's abstract or summary (it reports distractor efficiency and difficulty); the figure is removed until a source is found. The two arXiv papers do not conflict, as round 1 said: Tang compares with and without balancing instructions, while 2601.05414 always included one. Whether human item writers skew was not measured in these papers.

### D09-004 Multiple correct answers or no correct answer
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (round 3: 1 fetched study with a small sample on exactly this flaw, 1 fetched study that counts only missing keys, 1 snippet-only study. By count this could reach strong, but the second source covers only part of the flaw and the third is second-hand, so it is held at moderate for the lead to decide.)
- Dated: 2024-2026. ChatGPT-4o in the Hong Kong study; GPT-4 (via ChatGPT Pro, knowledge to June 2024) in Ould Ismail 2026; zero-shot GPT models (versions not recovered) in the Springer set. No GPT-5, Claude or Gemini 2.5+ flaw count exists.
- Why it reads as AI: the model does not check each distractor against the stem and source, so a distractor can be right in another context.
- Fix: add a second pass, by a human or a check against the source, that tests every distractor; reject items with two defensible keys.
- Data: Hong Kong cohort (Law et al., BMC Med Educ 25:208, 8 Feb 2025; fetched; ChatGPT-4o, two sets of 100 best-of-five items, 24 analysed participants): 11 AI items had multiple or no correct answers, against 1 human item, the largest gap in its flaw table. Overall item-writing flaws were similar (37 vs 35 per 100); other counts per 100 AI vs human items: factual errors 6 vs 4, irrelevance 6 vs 0, inappropriate difficulty 14 vs 1, duplicates 14 vs 7. Ould Ismail et al. (Academic Pathology 13(3):100275, 17 Jun 2026; fetched; GPT-4; 260 clinical-pathology MCQs): of 66 discarded items, 7 had a missing correct answer (about 2.7% of all 260, gatherer's calculation); the summary does not give a multiple-key count. Grévisse et al. 2024 (SN Computer Science, search only, summary from a citing paper): in computer science, ambiguous keys were the most common flaw; in the 100-item medicine sub-study (14 flawed items), implausible distractors came first, followed by no key, wrong key and ambiguous key. Round 1's "55 of 150 items had at least one flaw" is not confirmed. Advances in Medical Education and Practice 2026 rated "single correct answer" but reported no count (indirect).
- Sources: S-D09-004, S-D09-047, S-D09-002, S-D09-036
- Flag: small Hong Kong sample; the Springer source is snippet-level; the two fetched rates differ (11 per 100 vs about 2.7 per 100), which fits different definitions and models. No source argues this is fine.

### D09-005 Weak, implausible or non-functioning distractors
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (round 3: upgraded from anecdotal. One fetched study with measured student-choice data shows a significant AI deficit, and three more studies agree but were seen only as search summaries. A fetched RCT is in the same direction but not significant, and the fetched Hong Kong table points the other way on a different measure, so the evidence is mixed. The gatherer would have kept it anecdotal; the lead should confirm.) Lead check 21 Oct (r3): moderate accepted as '1 with good data' (Knight 2026, a measured significant result), but the evidence is mixed. The checklist should say 'check distractors with real students' rather than claim AI distractors are always weaker.
- Dated: 2024-2026 studies on ChatGPT, GPT-4, Copilot (GPT-4 based, March 2024) and DeepSeek R1; the 2026 RCT found no significant distractor deficit.
- Why it reads as AI: distractors are written in one pass with no model of the misconceptions real students hold.
- Fix: write distractors from known student misconceptions; after a live run, rewrite options chosen by under 5% of students.
- Data: Knight, McGee & Malau-Aduli (J Med Educ Curric Dev, 3 Mar 2026; PMC12957612; fetched; Copilot based on GPT-4, March 2024; 25 AI and 25 human items; 159 students): non-functioning distractors (chosen by under 5%) in 47% of AI vs 35% of human distractors (P = .04); all four distractors functioned in 12% of AI items vs 36% of human items; functioning distractors per item 2.12 vs 2.72 (P = .048). The paper reports distractor function, not a flaw rate. Medical Teacher 2025 (Wu et al., GPT-4 vs novice and expert authors; search only): distractor efficiency 39% for AI vs 55% for novices (p = 0.035), no difference from experts at 48% (p = 0.382); sample not recovered. Companion Medical Teacher paper (29 May 2025; 124 MCQs: 40 GPT-4, 39 novice, 45 expert; search only): AI items were judged unfit or in need of major revision more often than expert items. Dhanvijay et al. 2025 (Adv Physiol Educ 49:1045-1051; DeepSeek R1 physiology items vs faculty; search only): AI items easier, similar discrimination, more non-functional distractors. Ophthalmology resident exam (ChatGPT vs faculty; search only): 56% of ChatGPT items had item-writing flaws against 27% of faculty items; distractor quality higher in human items (p = 0.006); counts not recovered. Avicenna J Med (19 Nov 2025; PMC12747746; fetched) has distractor efficiency "Low" in 68% of AI vs 36% of expert items in Table 4, but its abstract, text and tables disagree (see D09-014), so it is not counted. Counter, fetched: 2026 RCT (BMC Medical Education 26:1376; 258 first-year medical students; items generated by "ChatGPT-4", a version the paper does not give): distractor efficiency 32.6% for AI vs 39.8% for student items, p = 0.070, not significant, same direction. The same trial found lower discrimination for AI items (see D09-014). Counter: Hong Kong (ChatGPT-4o) had 3 implausible distractors in AI items vs 7 in human items.
- Sources: S-D09-045, S-D09-008, S-D09-040, S-D09-039, S-D09-009, S-D09-010, S-D09-004, S-D09-046
- Flag: evidence is mixed; the only fetched supporting study is small (25 items per arm) and GPT-4 era, and three of the four supporters were seen only as search summaries. This is a general item-writing flaw, not clearly AI-specific. The Wu result holds against novices only.

### D09-006 "All of the above" and "None of the above" filler options
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: anecdotal (no source shows AI overuses these; samples show them rare)
- Dated: item-writing guidance against all-of-the-above is long-standing; ChatGPT-4o and 2024-era service samples show low use. Round 3 added no flaw rate for generated items.
- Why it reads as AI: these options are a cheap way to pad the option set; the evidence suggests current models rarely do this.
- Fix: avoid all-of-the-above; use none-of-the-above only when tested; write a full set of real distractors instead.
- Data: Hong Kong (Law et al. 2025, ChatGPT-4o, 100 items per set): all-of-the-above appeared 0 times in either set; none-of-the-above once in the AI set. Springer 2024 (zero-shot GPT models; sample not confirmed): all-of-the-above rarely generated, and no service in the first case study produced none-of-the-above (snippet only). A separate paper (Tam et al., arXiv 2503.01550, 28 LLMs; search only) shows accuracy dropping 30-50% on MMLU items where none-of-the-above is the key; that measures answering, not writing. The same paper counts 352 of 14,042 MMLU questions (about 2.5%) with a none-of-the-above option, a benchmark rate, not a generation rate. Bialik and Cummings (Springer 2026; search only) list all/none of the above as a checked flaw, with no rate.
- Sources: S-D09-004, S-D09-002, S-D09-011, S-D09-044
- Flag: the sources do not support this as an AI tell; they show the options are rare in AI items. Kept as a low-frequency check. Label is Avoid for all-of-the-above; none-of-the-above could be Choose deliberately.

### D09-007 Recall-only items and flat or easy difficulty
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (1 fetched study with good data, plus snippet-only support; the one newer-model study disagrees in part, so mixed)
- Dated: 2023-2026. Hong Kong used ChatGPT-4o; the one GPT-5 study (2026) saw no recall skew, but it measures match to a requested level, so it does not show the default skew has gone.
- Why it reads as AI: default prompts produce definitional recall items that students find easy.
- Fix: specify the Bloom level per item, require a scenario for apply and analyse items, and check the level mix and item difficulty after a live trial.
- Data: Hong Kong (Law et al. 2025; fetched; ChatGPT-4o, 100 items per set, 24 analysed participants): Bloom levels remember / understand / apply / analyse were 47 / 37 / 13 / 3 for AI vs 28 / 28 / 26 / 18 for human (chi-square = 14.27, p = 0.003); AI items were mostly remember and understand, and humans wrote more apply and analyse items. AI items were easier: difficulty index 0.78 vs 0.69 (p < 0.01), confirmed in the full text in round 3, with discrimination 0.22 vs 0.26 (p = 0.25). Inappropriate difficulty was flagged in 14 AI items vs 1 human item. Algorithm Aversion paper (2026): students solved 77% of LLM-generated MCQs vs 48% of human ones (sample and model not recovered, snippet only). Anatomy paper (Elzayyat et al.): expert questions rated highest for deeper analytical thinking (snippet only). Dhanvijay et al. 2025 (DeepSeek R1, physiology; search only): AI items easier than faculty items. Counter: Medişoğlu, Örs & Öçbe (Anatomy 20(2):112-128, 2026; fetched; GPT-5; 50 head-and-neck MCQs): 46 of 50 (92.0%) matched the intended Bloom level per experts (kappa 0.900), the other 4 landing lower than intended; scientific accuracy averaged 4.55 of 5. Counter, unverified: a 2026 hematology study reported 78% / 92% / 80% higher-order items for ChatGPT / DeepSeek / Perplexity (versions not recovered; the page needs a login, so round 1's figures stand unchecked). A 2024 social-science study found 60-74% of questions matched the intended Bloom level. Mitra et al. 2026 (fetched) found its Bloom rubric criterion "showed no effect or agreement"; that does not measure level mix, so it is not a counter.
- Sources: S-D09-004, S-D09-012, S-D09-013, S-D09-014, S-D09-015, S-D09-036, S-D09-037, S-D09-039
- Flag: mixed by model and prompt; the Hong Kong difficulty and Bloom figures are now confirmed from the full text, but the other supporting numbers are search snippets. The GPT-5 risk looks like items landing below the requested level, which is still worth a check.

### D09-008 Praise in quiz feedback ("Great question!", "Good work")
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (round 2: upgraded from anecdotal on 1 fetched study with measured counts; it tested one old model and measures praise in feedback, not specifically openers.) Lead review 20 Oct (r2): moderate accepted on one fetched study (GPT-4-0613, 2025). Round 3: narrowed, not upgraded. Current-model data (fetched, general chat) show excess praise depends heavily on the domain, low on factual tasks and high on open-ended or judgement tasks, so the tell is now scoped to open-ended feedback and recheck is still needed on a current quiz-feedback model. Held at moderate on the one education study; the gatherer suggested "moderate-to-anecdotal".
- Dated: the phrase became a meme in 2025-2026; the one measured quiz-feedback study used GPT-4-0613 in 2025. No GPT-5, Claude or Gemini quiz-feedback praise rate has been measured. The 2026 current-model study is general chat, not education.
- Why it reads as AI: assistants are rewarded for being warm and agreeable, so feedback arrives wrapped in praise whether or not the answer earns it.
- Fix: state why the key is right and name the learner's specific error; no praise openers; check outputs with a simple pattern filter, and check open-ended feedback hardest.
- Data: Herklotz, Ippisch & Haensch (LMU Munich, arXiv 2511.04213v1, 6 Nov 2025; fetched): GPT-4-0613 via StudyLabs on a German Introduction to Statistics mock exam; praise appeared in 68% of 276 manually coded feedback instances, across all performance levels. This stays the education-specific figure, dated to the model it tested. The paper gives no separate praise rate for wrong answers; the authors only note that the model keeps flattering when answers are wrong, and link this to sycophancy. Vennemeyer et al., "Sycophantic Praise: Evaluating Excessive Praise in Language Models" (arXiv 2606.07441, 5 Jun 2026; fetched): general chat with synthetic personas, not educational feedback, though two K-12 teachers validated it. Excess praise by its SyPr metric: GPT-5.4 15.1%, Claude Sonnet 4.6 12.0%, Qwen3 30B 29.0%, DeepSeek V4 Flash 32.3%. Claude Sonnet 4.6 showed 41.9% on moral reasoning against 1.3% on MMLU Economics and 0.3% on MMLU Chemistry. Human labels: GPT-5.4 17.0%, Claude 18.6%; Claude on Economics 3.8%, Chemistry 5.7%, moral reasoning 29.6%. Per the gatherer, adding "What do you think?" raised rates for all models. Reading: in current models, praise is rare on factual tasks and common on judgement tasks, and no study has measured how a current model praises feedback on a quiz answer. Bo et al. (arXiv 2510.03667, Oct 2025; fetched): 24 undergraduate machine-learning novices, chatbots on GPT-4.1, a debugging task rather than quizzes; confidence-weighted accuracy fell with the high-sycophancy bot (beta = -21.58, p < .0001), count-based accuracy did not differ significantly, and F1 gains were 49.29% (low-sycophancy) vs 4.78% (high). Round 1's "improved less on mental-model measures" overstated this. Adjacent (search only): Li, Lan & Hu (Educational Technology Research and Development, 2025) compared GPT-4.0 and teacher feedback on student-written questions; GPT feedback used frequent praise and students preferred teacher feedback. Indirect: a 557-participant study found unmodified LLM behaviour suppressed discovery and inflated confidence (arXiv 2602.14270, search only). The phrase itself rests on a weak slang blog.
- Sources: S-D09-019, S-D09-048, S-D09-017, S-D09-049, S-D09-018, S-D09-016
- Flag: Herklotz measures brief praise in feedback, not praise as an opening line; one study, one 2023-era model, 276 coded instances. The sycophancy studies are not about quizzes, and the current-model study suggests the rate may be low for factual tasks. The Li, Lan & Hu and phrase sources are search only (the phrase source is a weak blog). The gatherer's remark that full-score students were praised more often is unchecked and left out.

### D09-009 Generic or shallow explanations that do not name the misconception
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (round 2: upgraded from anecdotal on 2 fetched studies, but they show generic and thin feedback, not restating. The original restate-the-answer claim has no direct evidence. Counter-evidence exists. Round 3 added one fetched older-model data point; no change.)
- Dated: 2025 studies on GPT-4o and GPT-4-0613, and a 2023 ChatGPT-3.5 study; not tested on 2026 models in a quiz setting.
- Why it reads as AI: the default explanation covers the content the model has, or paraphrases the key, rather than the reasoning the learner got wrong, so it looks thorough and misses the point.
- Fix: the explanation must say why the key is right, and why each distractor is wrong, naming the misconception; reject feedback that names none; keep explanations short and specific.
- Data: Physics tutoring dialogues (arXiv 2504.00408, April 2025; fetched; GPT-4o-2024-08-06): graders found the model often failed to identify and address the student's specific misconception, giving long general walkthroughs, and it rarely asked students to explain their reasoning first; interactions were rated about 55% clearly explaining the key concept, 25% touching it, 9% not addressing it and 11% inaccurate. Herklotz et al. (arXiv 2511.04213v1; fetched; GPT-4-0613; statistics mock exam): 97% of feedback instances explained why an answer was right or wrong (43% of the text) but conceptually focused feedback appeared in 44% of instances (21% of the text); the denominator was not confirmed; students with the same answer sometimes got different levels of conceptual explanation; 6.99% of 2,389 feedback instances (n = 167) contained at least one error. Ngo et al. (Academic Pathology 11(1):100099, Dec 2023; fetched; ChatGPT-3.5, 60 immunology questions): of the 26 items needing substantial modification, 19 lacked explanations for the wrong options and 4 had too little detail; this supports the "why each distractor is wrong" half of the Fix, on an older model. Students in a practice-exam study wanted a short why-right-or-wrong summary and disliked thin feedback (arXiv 2505.13381, search only). Counter: an LLM feedback study (arXiv 2601.15280) reported learning equal to educator feedback with better student perceptions (details not recovered); a 2025 Computers and Education: AI study found pedagogical quality comparable to human feedback, with weak metacognition support; a 2026 tutoring study (Thomas, Borchers, Koedinger et al.) found learners rated LLM feedback helpful, with learning gains in 2 of 7 lessons (both counters search summaries only).
- Sources: S-D09-034, S-D09-019, S-D09-024, S-D09-035, S-D09-020, S-D09-042, S-D09-043
- Flag: no study tests "restating" explicitly; the tell was reframed around generic or thin feedback, which the evidence supports better. Physics tutoring is dialogue, not quiz explanations. All fetched studies used older models. Round 1's D09-009 sources claimed "most feedback explained right or wrong" from a snippet; this is now confirmed from the full text.

### D09-010 Confident factual errors and wrong keys
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: strong (round 3: restored from moderate. Three independent fetched primary studies from different teams report errors or wrong keys: Ngo 2023, Law 2025, Ould Ismail 2026. Round 2's downgrade was because the key study (Camarata) and the main systematic review were search-only; they now count only as extra support. The PLOS One meta-analysis pools overlapping studies and is not a separate origin. It is NOT strong for 'more than human items': Law's 6% vs 4% is close. The sharper gap is items with multiple or no correct answers (11 vs 1 in Law).
- Dated: 2023-2026. Studies mostly tested ChatGPT-3.5, GPT-4 and GPT-4o. No GPT-5, Claude or Gemini 2.5+ factual-error rate was found, so there is no evidence on whether rates fall as models improve either way. No source supports unchecked use.
- Why it reads as AI: the model states false facts or wrong keys in the same confident tone as correct ones, and has nothing to check them against.
- Fix: trace every key to a source passage the teacher supplies, and have a human verify each key; a second model's check is not enough.
- Data: Ngo et al. ### D09-011 Repetitive templates across a set of items
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: anecdotal (lead review 20 Oct: downgraded from moderate; the evidence is indirect. Round 3 added one fetched indirect study and one fetched nearby count, and no change was made.)
- Dated: 2024-2026; GPT-4o-mini in the math study, 7-9B open models in the KDD study, other models not recovered.
- Why it reads as AI: prompt-driven generation converges on one stem skeleton and one cognitive operation.
- Fix: keep a list of item types and skeletons, reject near-duplicate stems by n-gram or embedding similarity, and use varied few-shot examples.
- Data: school-level zero-shot question generation produced repetitive questions and an eight-example prompt broadened the set (sample and model not recovered). GPT-4o-mini math items varied mostly in coefficients and symbols, keeping the same cognitive operation (arXiv 2511.11635, sample not recovered). A 2024 study found LLM distractors capturing student misconceptions were limited and suggested varied in-context examples (arXiv 2405.00864). Round 3, fetched: "From Memorization to Creation" (arXiv 2606.18257, 6 May 2026, KDD '26) scored how far generated questions differ from the seed question on a "Unique" score; baseline chain-of-thought scores in computer science were GLM-4-9B 90.8, Qwen2.5-7B 74.6, Baichuan2-7B 85.3, LLaMA3-8B 97.2, InternLM3-8B 94.8, Spark 97.0, and a fine-grained prompt raised Qwen2.5-7B to 99.0 (higher reads as more distinct, inferred from the lift). Generators were 7-9B open models plus one closed model (Spark); no GPT-5, Claude or Gemini was measured. Law et al. 2025 (fetched; ChatGPT-4o): duplicates in 14 of 100 AI items vs 7 of 100 human items; the meaning of "duplicate" was not checked, so it may not be the same as template repetition.
- Sources: S-D09-026, S-D09-027, S-D09-028, S-D09-050, S-D09-004
- Flag: indirect evidence only. The KDD study measures similarity to a seed question, not repetition across a shipped bank, and the Law count is a nearby measure. No study measured stem-template repetition in a shipped quiz bank. Three of the five sources are search only.

### D09-012 Negative stems ("Which is NOT ...")
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: anecdotal (round 3: 1 study, now fetched, on an older model; a fetched newer study found none)
- Dated: noted from 2023 on ChatGPT-3.5 items; ChatGPT-4o items in 2025 showed none, so older-model finding.
- Why it reads as AI: models may default to negation for variety, which item-writing guidance discourages.
- Fix: state the positive form, or use a clear "which is the exception" stem.
- Data: Ngo et al. (Academic Pathology 11(1):100099, 2023; fetched; ChatGPT-3.5, 60 items): 3 of 60 items (5%) were phrased as negative questions and counted as needing revision. Mitra et al. 2026 (fetched) assess "stem worded positively" as a rubric criterion with no rates reported; Gemini and ChatGPT scored similarly high in pathology. Counter: Hong Kong (Law et al. 2025, ChatGPT-4o, 100 items per set; fetched) had 0 negative stems in both sets.
- Sources: S-D09-024, S-D09-004, S-D09-036
- Flag: one older-model source at 5% of items, contradicted by a fetched newer study.

### D09-013 Flashcards that lack context, accept several answers, or run wordy
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (round 2: upgraded from anecdotal; the primary Memory Machines report was fetched and gives user-rated counts. One report plus one preprint seen as a search summary.)
- Dated: 2025-2026; the only study here that tested 2025-26 models (GPT-5.2 best of 11 generators, plus Gemini 2.5 Pro and 3, Claude Opus 4.5 and Sonnet 4.5).
- Why it reads as AI: the model does not judge whether a card will still make sense out of context or on review months later, so many cards fail as spaced-repetition prompts.
- Fix: one fact per card; test each card on its own and delete any whose answer needs the source text; sample-check cards against the source for invented facts.
- Data: Memory Machines report (Kirkby & Matuschak, 2026; fetched at the primary page): 42 spaced-repetition users rated about 1,500 prompts made from 93 sources by 11 generator models (the headline and arena pages cite 16 to 19 models, not reconciled). GPT-5.2 was the best generator, yet 36% of its prompts were unusable (10% off-target, 26% needing refactor); "unusable" means prompts that would not survive long-run review. The report's failure categories are lacks context, multiple responses, shallow, wordy and narrow; it has no "hallucinated" or "ambiguous" category and gives no 36% split by category. No LLM judge exceeded 70% accuracy on usable vs unusable; grounding the judge with rated prompts lifted Sonnet 4.5's precision from 56% to 78%. Arena figures (search only): usable GPT-5.2 64.3%, Claude Sonnet 5 58.0% (added after the report), Claude Opus 4.6 52.8%, Gemini 2.5 Pro 52.1%, Claude Opus 4.5 49.8%. Brown University preprint (medRxiv 10.1101/2025.05.13.25327518, 2025; 143 first-year students; flashcards and summaries from 20 lectures; model not recovered; search summary only): an optimised pipeline produced 1 hallucinated flashcard in 21 (4.8%), covered 100% of the faculty's learning objectives, and exam scores did not differ significantly (genetics p = 0.86, pharmacology p = 0.05); 74% said the cards saved time (self-report).
- Sources: S-D09-032, S-D09-033, S-D09-029, S-D09-030
- Flag: one report, not peer reviewed, by authors who run spaced-repetition projects. The Brown figures come from a search summary of the primary preprint; it is unclear whether "1 in 21" counts cards or lecture sets. The Brown result shows cards that were usable and did not hurt scores, so it partly cuts against the tell. Round 1's wording "ambiguous, wordy, context-dependent rather than hallucinated" is not in the report. A blog's "31% of cards needed editing" was not found in the preprint and is not used.

### D09-014 Weak item discrimination in AI-written items
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: moderate (1 fetched peer-reviewed RCT with good data, plus one more fetched item analysis in the same direction; one fetched study found no difference and two search-only studies agree with it, so the evidence is mixed) Lead review 20 Oct (r2): moderate accepted on one blinded RCT, which counts as good data. Treat it as "check discrimination after a pilot", not as a settled deficit. The items were made with ChatGPT-4 in Dec 2024. Round 3 added Knight (fetched) and a caution-only Avicenna; no second RCT confirmed.
- Dated: exam held Dec 2024, published 17 Jun 2026; generator "ChatGPT-4" (the paper does not say GPT-4 or GPT-4o). Knight used Copilot (GPT-4 based, March 2024). The counter-studies used GPT-4 (Medical Teacher 2025), ChatGPT-4o (Hong Kong) and DeepSeek R1 (Dhanvijay 2025). No gap is shown for a GPT-5, Claude or Gemini 2.5+ generator.
- Why it reads as AI: a generator that writes plausible, uniformly easy items produces little spread in scores, so the exam separates strong students from weak ones less well. This is psychometric, not visible in a single item.
- Fix: check item discrimination after a pilot or live run; drop or rewrite items with poor discrimination; do not rely on difficulty alone.
- Data: BMC Medical Education 26:1376 (fetched): randomised trial, 258 first-year UBC MD students, participant-blinded; AI items had mean discrimination index 0.19 against 0.25 for student-written items; poor discrimination (index 0.20 or less) in 62.5% of AI items against 42.9% of student items. The text gives p = 0.0001 for the index comparison while Table 4 gives p = 0.002; it is significant either way. The authors call the gap "consistent with score compression" on an open-resource formative exam. Items per arm: 112 per the gatherer (not in the lead's spot check). AI items took far less time to write (4.2 vs 19.6 minutes). Distractor efficiency 32.6% vs 39.8% was not significant (p = 0.070). Knight, McGee & Malau-Aduli (PMC12957612; fetched; Copilot, GPT-4 based; 25 AI vs 25 human items; 159 students; not randomised): discrimination 0.19 vs 0.29 (t(45.7) = 2.21, P = .03); the text says 56% of AI items fell below 0.20, while Table 1 implies 24% for human items, and the text and table disagree. Avicenna J Med (19 Nov 2025; PMC12747746; fetched; 24 teachers wrote 386 AI-assisted and 506 expert MCQs; 42 students completed; Gemini, Llama, Copilot and ChatGPT, versions not given; 25 vs 25 items analysed): the abstract gives discrimination 0.32 vs 0.48 (P = .0082) but the Discussion gives 0.28 vs 0.34 (P = .02), and difficulty and distractor efficiency also differ between abstract, text and tables; use with caution. The gatherer found no "randomized, blinded" wording in the fetched text. JMIR 2026 (e100179; human-edited generative-AI MCQs for family medicine postgraduates; search only, both fetches empty): discrimination 0.09 vs 0.18 (mean difference -0.08, 95% CI -0.16 to -0.01, P = .04), but which group had which value was not confirmed. Counter: Law et al. 2025 (fetched; ChatGPT-4o): discrimination 0.22 vs 0.26 (P = .25), no significant difference, though optimal discrimination (index 0.3 or more) was reached in 38% of AI vs 51% of human items and KR-20 reliability was 0.75 vs 0.83; writing time was 24.5 vs 96 person-hours. Counter, search only: Medical Teacher 2025 (GPT-4 vs novice and expert authors) found no discrimination difference; Dhanvijay et al. 2025 (DeepSeek R1) reported similar discrimination.
- Sources: S-D09-010, S-D09-045, S-D09-046, S-D09-004, S-D09-051, S-D09-008, S-D09-039
- Flag: the RCT and Knight are single-centre or small and GPT-4 era; Avicenna's figures are internally inconsistent and the JMIR result is search only with unconfirmed groups. Net in fetched item analyses: gaps in 2 of 3 clean sources, none in Law. Do not read the shorter writing time as a reason to skip the check.

### D09-015 Format drift to fill-in-the-blank or true/false items
- Label: Avoid
- Confidence: anecdotal (round 3 candidate C1. One search-only source that lists the flaw in a rubric without a rate; a fetched study points the other way)
- Dated: 2026 rubric; no date for the rate. Generators in that source were gpt-4.1-nano and gemini-2.5-flash.
- Why it reads as AI: the model sometimes drifts from the requested multiple-choice format, so the item set looks inconsistent and teachers have to rewrite items.
- Fix: fix the format in the prompt and reject items whose stem is not a single-best-answer question; check the batch for format mix.
- Data: Bialik and Cummings, "Improving Multiple Choice Questions Using Synthetic Data" (Springer 2026; search only, full text behind a login redirect): the rubric flags fill-in-the-blank format and true/false degeneration among five checked flaws; per-flaw rates are not in the abstract. Counter: Frontiers 2026 (fetched; Mixtral-8x7B) found AI items used true/false far less often than teacher items (7.6% vs 25.4%).
- Sources: S-D09-044, S-D09-001
- Flag: a rubric that lists a flaw does not show the flaw occurs. Treat as a lead, not a tell; not in the checklist.

## Not a tell

### D09-N01 Clean grammar and spelling
- Why it is fine: error-free wording in items is a sign of care that human items often lack; it does not show weak content, and content still needs checking. Hong Kong table (ChatGPT-4o, 100 items per set): 0 grammar or spelling errors in AI items and 24 in human items.
- Sources: S-D09-004

### D09-N02 Standard option formatting and avoided true/false misuse
- Why it is fine: consistent, homogeneous options of about equal length are good item-writing practice, and AI items rarely misuse the true/false format (7.6% vs 25.4% in teacher-only items, Frontiers, Mixtral-8x7B). Equal option length does not conflict with D09-001, which is about a key that is longer than its distractors.
- Sources: S-D09-001, S-D09-031

### D09-N03 Cue flaws as proof of AI authorship
- Why it is fine: longest-option and word-repeat cues also occur in human items (34.9% each in teacher-only items), so they are item-writing habits to check for in every item, not an AI signature. The same Frontiers study found teacher-AI items worse than either source alone, so teacher review does not remove them; and Hong Kong human items had more implausible distractors (7) than AI items (3).
- Sources: S-D09-001, S-D09-004

### D09-N04 Students rate AI-written items as no worse
- Why it is fine: in the BMC Medical Education randomised trial (258 students, "ChatGPT-4" items), nine student-perception domains (clarity, relevance, quality, difficulty and others) showed no significant difference between AI and student items after Bonferroni correction (all adjusted p of at least 0.12). Students not noticing is not proof the items are sound: the same trial found the discrimination gap in D09-014, so the item data still needs checking.
- Sources: S-D09-010
